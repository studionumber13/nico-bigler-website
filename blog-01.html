ARTICLE 01 · JUNE 2026

The Other Side of the Deal

The second in a series exploring artist-brand collaborations.
The Brand Side: Buying Access, Not Design

A campaign communicates what a brand does. Art communicates what a brand believes.

That is why brands across every industry, from fashion and automotive to technology and food, are increasingly seeking out artists as strategic partners. Not for decoration. For meaning. And the conversation almost always focuses on one side. What I find more interesting is the other question: what does the artist actually get out of it?

When a brand approaches an artist, they are not buying a design. They are buying access to a cultural community they cannot reach on their own.

In saturated markets, differentiation through product alone has a ceiling. Art creates something that a campaign cannot: an identity that connects to culture rather than just commerce. The meaning that lives in an artist's world transfers into the brand's world. That changes what the product means to the people who buy it.

Adidas understood this when they signed Kanye West in 2013. At the time, Adidas was trailing Nike in almost every relevant market. West did not bring a design. He brought an entire cultural ecosystem: music, streetwear, contemporary art. All things Adidas could not have built organically. The Yeezy line eventually generated around 1.5 billion euros annually. Not because the shoes were technically superior. Because of what they meant to the people wearing them.

The Artist Side: Buying Scale, Not Selling Out

For an artist, a brand collaboration offers something the traditional art market rarely provides: scale.

When Keith Haring collaborated with Swatch in 1986, he was not compromising his practice. He was extending it. Haring believed art belongs outside galleries, on subway walls, on everyday objects, in the hands of people who would never set foot in a museum. Swatch operated from the same principle: the watch as the world's smallest canvas, a democratic object that anyone can wear.

They were not two parties negotiating a deal. They were two parallel ideas that finally met. That is the difference between a collaboration and a transaction. It is visible in the result every single time.

What I Actually Look For

If I could choose one brand to collaborate with today, it would be Stussy. The prints, the attitude, the way they built a global subculture out of a signature. That is exactly the kind of brand identity I find interesting to work with visually. But if I am honest: what made Stussy iconic was a specific era. The 90s. The decade between 2010 and 2020. Right now, in 2026, I am still waiting for a design that hits the same way.

That gap, between what a brand was and what it is putting out today, is itself an argument for why artist collaborations matter. Sometimes a brand needs an outside eye to find its way back to what made it worth caring about.

That is the question I ask before any creative partnership: do we believe the same things? If the answer is no, the scale does not matter.

When the Exchange Breaks Down

A brand that approaches an artist with a predefined brief and a fixed outcome is not collaborating. They are commissioning. The result might look interesting. But it will not carry the weight that makes these partnerships worth doing.

An artist who accepts any offer regardless of fit is not extending their practice. They are diluting it. And credibility, once lost in this space, is very hard to rebuild.

The collaborations that hold up share one characteristic: both parties brought something genuine, and both walked away with something they could not have built alone. Finding that alignment before committing is where most collaborations fail before they even begin.

And it raises a question worth sitting with: how do you actually know if the fit is real before you commit to it? Because the signals are there. You just have to know where to look.
